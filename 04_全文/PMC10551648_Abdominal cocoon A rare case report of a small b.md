## Introduction

Sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis (SEP), or abdominal cocoon, is a remarkably rare entity. It consists of a fibrous layer or cocoon-like sac encasing, in most cases, the small bowel. The lack of specific symptoms makes preoperative diagnosis challenging.

## Case presentation

A 50-year-old patient, with no history of abdominal surgery or medical diseases, was admitted for symptoms of small bowel obstruction. On examination, the patient was underweight with a BMI of 18 kg/m2. Vital signs were stable. His abdomen was mildly distended and soft. Careful adhesiolysis with complete resection of the membrane was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful. One year later, the patient was symptom-free.

## Conclusion

SEP is an uncommon cause of bowel obstruction. Preoperative detection can be challenging in the absence of pathognomonic signs. Treatment is based on surgery, specifically entailing complete excision of the cocoon and adhesiolysis.

## Introduction

Sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis (SEP), or abdominal cocoon, is a rare condition, first reported in 1907 by Owtschinnikow [1]. It is defined as a thick fibro-collagenous membrane encasing the intestinal bowel and possibly other abdominal organs [2]. According to the underlying cause, SEP is classified into primary or idiopathic and secondary SEP. The clinical picture of primary SEP ranges from asymptomatic patients to those with recurring bouts of small bowel obstruction [3]. In this paper, we report a case of small bowel obstruction secondary to a primary SEP with initial imaging suggesting internal hernia. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [4].

## Case presentation

A 50-year-old patient, with no history of abdominal surgery or medical diseases, was admitted to our general surgery department for symptoms of small bowel obstruction evolving for 12 h. These symptoms included abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal distension. Throughout the previous year, the patient experienced similar episodes but never sought medical attention because of spontaneous resolution of symptoms. On examination, the patient had no fever, a blood pressure of 120 / 70 mmHg and a heart rate of 95 beats per minute. The patient was underweight with a BMI of 18 kg/m2. His abdomen was mildly distended and soft without tenderness or any other signs suggesting peritonitis. The rectum was empty upon examination. Laboratory findings showed an elevated white blood cell count without renal function impairment or electrolyte disorders. A biological nutritional assessment revealed low levels of serum albumin, while all other parameters fell within the normal range. An abdominal CT scan was performed, showing a bottle gourd sign with a dilated duodenum associated with a feces sign. The jejunum was collapsed. These findings were in favor of a left paraduodenal hernia with incarcerated small bowel loops (Fig. 1). There were no signs of ischemia or perforation. After admission, the patient was kept for 6 h under conservative management. He had appropriate fluid resuscitation and was kept nil per os under suction using a nasogastric tube. Parenteral nutritional supplements were administered. Evolution was favorable, with complete resolution of small bowel obstruction syndrome. The patient underwent a scheduled laparotomy ten days later. Intraoperative findings excluded the diagnosis of an internal hernia. The small bowels were encapsulated in a cocoon-like structure formed by a thick fibrous tissue associated with multiple adhesions (Fig. 2). Careful adhesiolysis with complete resection of the membrane were performed (Supplementary video). The review of the CT scan after surgery allowed for the identification of the Cocoon (Fig. 3). The postoperative course was uneventful. The anatomopathological report showed fibrous connective tissue with a collagen reaction. The diagnosis of primary SEP was retained after excluding tuberculosis and sarcoidosis based on the pathology report, the absence of a chronic drug use, and the absence of signs of cirrhosis according to CT and intraoperative findings. The patient remained asymptomatic after a 1-year follow-up.

## Discussion

SEP is a rare condition responsible for partial or complete encapsulation of intraperitoneal organs (including the small bowel, large bowel, and possibly abdominal viscera) [2]. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, SEP is thought to be a persistent inflammatory disorder due to a cause that remains unidentified. It starts with recurring latent peritonitis with no specific abdominal symptoms, evolving to sclerosis and membrane formation, thus forming a cocoon encasing the intestinal bowel [5]. Given that SEP is an uncommon disease, and its etiologies are diverse, the overall incidence and prevalence of this entity are yet to be determined [6]. According to the latest literature review conducted in 2022 by Chorti et al. [7], only 240 SEP cases were published throughout the international literature. SEP is divided into two categories based on its primum movens: primary (idiopathic) and secondary (acquired). Primary SEP was found to be less common, with no exact scientific evidence of an existing etiology underneath [2,8]. Studies conducted on peritoneal fibrosis and neoangiogenesis, suggested a vast myriad of possible mechanisms leading to primary SEP (e.g.: retrograde menstruation, retrograde peritonitis, omentum hypoplasia, and mesenteric vessel defect) [9,10]. More case studies are required to clarify the exact cause of idiopathic SEP. Secondary SEP's pathophysiology is currently better established as a local or systemic factor that sets in motion peritoneal inflammation. Among these factors, the most implicated in secondary SEP is peritoneal dialysis, with its duration identified as an independent predictor of secondary SEP [11]. Other etiologies were also identified, such as systemic diseases (tuberculosis and sarcoidosis), long-term use of b-blockers, gynecological tumors (ovarian thecomas), cirrhosis, and transplanting organs [12]. Based on scientific papers, >50 % of SEP patients were admitted to emergency departments for symptoms of bowel obstruction [7]. This clinical presentation is the direct result of bowel compression and struggle in the limited space formed within the cocoon [13]. In most cases, these are recurrent symptoms that last several months before an urgent episode leads to emergency counseling. As for our case, our patient experienced multiple episodes of subocclusion over one year before seeking medical attention. In the largest survey conducted on primary SEP, the median symptom duration prior to emergency presentation was around 4 years, and because of this disease's chronic evolution, almost 75 % of patients were malnourished [9]. Abdominal masses and ascites were also described in some case series [14]. Even after a thorough investigation of the patient's history and a complete physical examination, due to the lack of specific symptoms in the early stages of SEP, preoperative diagnosis is challenging [15]. Until this day, no specific biomarker of SEP has been identified [16], with laboratory findings only in favor of infection or inflammation [17]. Given the limited number of documented cases, no consensus over the most effective approach for diagnosing SEP was ever reached, but contrast-enhanced computed tomography appears to be the imaging technique of choice [5]. The most distinctive radiological sign is the aggregation of small bowel segments at the midline, encased by a thick capsule (>2 mm) with a contrast-free border [18]. Other signs can be observed, such as the bottle gourd sign, ascites, increased fat density in the great omentum, and peritoneal calcifications. In our case, we found the bottle gourd sign preoperatively. The cocoon was identified after reviewing the images postoperatively. Despite an extensive clinical and radiological assessment, <30 % of SEPs are diagnosed in the preoperative phase, with mainly two conditions to consider as differential diagnoses: internal hernia and congenital peritoneal encapsulation [7].

The most common mode of SEP revelation is bowel obstruction, and because there is no clear consensus on SEP management, the therapeutic approach typically follows the management strategy of bowel obstruction. Conservative treatment based on fluid resuscitation, bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, and correct nutritional support is reserved for patients without signs of vital distress [19]. For patients suffering from SEP, nutritional status should not be taken lightly. It is crucial to improve the nutritional status of SEP patients since it may enhance their responsiveness to conservative therapy and avoid post-operative events such as infection and leaks [5].
